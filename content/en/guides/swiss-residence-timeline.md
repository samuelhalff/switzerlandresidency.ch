Moving to Switzerland follows a fairly stable sequence: tax pre-assessment, a ruling where relevant, the permit application, federal approval for most non-EU routes, then arrival, registration and the practical deadlines that follow. How long each step takes is another matter: no official processing time is published for the main non-EU routes, and practice differs from canton to canton. This guide sets out the order, the legal deadlines that do exist, and what tends to speed things up or slow them down.

**Key facts (as of September 2026)**

| | |
|---|---|
| Official processing times | None published for the non-EU retiree or fiscal-interest routes |
| Non-EU permit decision | The canton decides; SEM approval mandatory for the retiree and fiscal-interest routes |
| Lump-sum amount | Agreed with the canton before or on arrival |
| Commune registration | Within 14 days of entry (non-employed stays over 3 months with an entry permit) |
| Health insurance | Within 3 months of registering (B or C permit) |
| Driving licence | Swiss licence needed after 12 months' residence |
| Family reunification (non-EU) | Within 5 years; children over 12 within 12 months |

## Why nobody can give you a firm date

Most of the steps depend on decisions by others: the cantonal tax administration, the cantonal migration office and, for many non-EU routes, the State Secretariat for Migration (SEM). The non-EU routes for people without work are "may" provisions, which means the authorities have wide discretion. We do not know of any published official timeline for them. So we plan with you around the steps and the order, and we keep a margin for the parts no one controls.

## The sequence, step by step

### 1. Tax pre-assessment

Before anything is filed, you need a clear picture of your tax position: which cantons are realistic, whether lump-sum taxation fits (it needs a first-time Swiss tax liability or 10+ years' absence, and no work in Switzerland for either spouse), and how ordinary taxation would compare. This is also when your adviser at home looks at exit rules and the timing of your departure. When you become tax-resident is explained in our guide to [Swiss tax residency](/en/guides/swiss-tax-residency/).

### 2. Tax ruling or lump-sum agreement

If you choose lump-sum taxation, the amount is agreed with the canton **before or on arrival**. A ruling is a written, advance assessment by the cantonal tax authority of a concrete set of facts that is about to happen. It binds the authority under good faith if the conditions are met. How this interacts with the permit differs:

- In **Vaud**, a signed lump-sum agreement with the cantonal tax administration is one of the documents for the non-EU fiscal-interest permit.
- In **Geneva**, the lump-sum request goes in writing to the tax administration together with the migration procedure, with a proposed amount and a lifestyle form.
- In **Zurich**, which has no lump-sum regime, the fiscal-interest permit needs the tax authority's confirmation of the expected tax.

See [getting a tax ruling before you move](/en/guides/tax-ruling-before-moving/).

### 3. Permit application

The route depends on nationality:

- **EU/EFTA nationals without work** need sufficient financial means and comprehensive health and accident insurance. There are no quotas. The permit is normally valid for 5 years.
- **Non-EU nationals** without work usually apply as retirees (from age 55, with special personal ties to Switzerland and sufficient means) or on the grounds of significant cantonal fiscal interests, moving their centre of life here.

The canton examines the file and decides at its discretion. Our guide to [residence for financially independent non-EU nationals](/en/guides/residence-non-eu-financially-independent/) covers the conditions.

### 4. SEM approval (non-EU routes)

For the retiree and fiscal-interest routes, **SEM approval is mandatory**. The canton tells you it intends to approve, then sends the case to SEM, which may refuse, limit the permit or attach conditions. This is a separate step with its own review, and it is one reason why no one can promise a date.

### 5. Entry and registration

Once approved, you enter Switzerland and register with your commune. For non-employed people staying longer than three months with an entry permit, registration is due **within 14 days of entry** (art. 10 VZAE). Many later deadlines count from this date.

### 6. The first three months

Basic health insurance must be taken out **within three months of registering** (art. 7 para. 1 KVV). If you are on time, cover is backdated to registration. In the same period, most families register for AHV/AVS as non-employed residents, open a bank account, and import household goods and cars. Goods and vehicles enter duty-free as relocation goods if you have used them personally for at least six months and keep using them. Our [first 90 days checklist](/en/guides/first-90-days-checklist/) goes through this in order.

### 7. The first year

A Swiss driving licence is needed once you have lived here for **12 months** without a break of more than three months abroad (art. 42 para. 3bis VZV). If you are on a lump sum, you report any change in your situation to the tax authority without being asked.

### 8. Later milestones

- **Family reunification (non-EU):** requests must be made within **5 years**, and for children over 12 within **12 months** (art. 47 AIG).
- **C permit:** after 5 years as of right for nationals of countries with a settlement agreement; possible after 5 years, at the canton's discretion, for others including UK, US and Canadian nationals; otherwise after 10 years (art. 34 AIG).

## The sequence at a glance

| Step | Who decides | Deadline or rule |
|---|---|---|
| Tax pre-assessment | You and your advisers | None; start early |
| Ruling or lump-sum agreement | Cantonal tax administration | Before or on arrival |
| Permit application | Cantonal migration office | No published processing time |
| SEM approval (non-EU routes) | SEM | Mandatory for retiree and fiscal-interest routes |
| Registration | Commune | Within 14 days of entry |
| Health insurance | You, with an insurer | Within 3 months of registration |
| Driving licence | Cantonal road traffic office | After 12 months' residence |

## What speeds things up

- **A complete file from the start.** Missing documents lead to back-and-forth. Civil-status documents, proof of means and insurance, and translations take time to gather.
- **The tax position settled first.** Where the canton wants the lump-sum agreement in the permit file, a file without it cannot move.
- **A clear choice of canton.** Changing canton midway can mean starting the tax and permit steps again.
- **Renting first.** Buying a home is not needed for a permit, and owning property gives no right to one. A lease lets you register while you look for the right house.

## What slows things down

- **Discretion and scrutiny.** On the non-EU routes, fiscal interest is weighed against Switzerland's reputation. Federal case law has said that admitting some applicants for purely fiscal reasons could harm Switzerland's standing, for example in a sanctions context.
- **Work that does not fit the route.** Lump-sum taxation and the retiree route both exclude gainful activity in Switzerland. Board seats or advisory roles need to be looked at early.
- **Timing at home.** Exit rules, the end of a tax year or the sale of a business may dictate when you can leave.
- **School admissions.** Places at popular international schools follow their own calendar, which may not match yours.
