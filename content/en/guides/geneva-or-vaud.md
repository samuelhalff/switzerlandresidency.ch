Geneva and Vaud share the same lake, the same language and many of the same international families, but they are different places to live and they tax newcomers differently. Geneva is a compact city-canton with a strong international presence; Vaud stretches from Lausanne and the lakeside towns up into the Alps. For families considering lump-sum taxation, the two cantons calculate the base, cover wealth tax and treat inheritances in distinct ways, so the choice deserves a proper comparison.

**Key facts (as of September 2026)**

| | Geneva | Vaud |
|---|---|---|
| Lump-sum cantonal minimum 2026 | CHF 426,357 | CHF 415,000 (includes a 15% wealth surcharge) |
| Wealth tax under the lump sum | +10% surcharge on the base | Included in the base |
| Rent test | 7× rent or rental value | 7× rent **+10%** |
| Non-EU fiscal-interest permit | Published expenditure base of CHF 750,000 | No amount published; signed lump-sum agreement required |
| Inheritance to children | Exempt, except if the deceased was lump-sum taxed recently | CHF 1 m exempt, then a degressive deduction up to CHF 2 m |
| Federal minimum (both) | CHF 435,000 | CHF 435,000 |

## The short answer

If you want city life, very short distances and the international organisations on your doorstep, Geneva is the natural fit. If you want more space, a choice between town, lake and mountains, or a particular school outside Geneva, Vaud gives you more options. On tax, neither is simply "cheaper": it depends on your figures, and for lump-sum families on inheritance plans too.

## Two different places to live

**Geneva** is a small canton, largely built around the city and its lakeside, with French as the official language. Much of international life in the region is concentrated here, and Geneva's airport serves both cantons.

**Vaud** is much larger. Lausanne is its main city, and the canton runs along the northern shore of the lake through towns such as Nyon, Rolle, Morges, Vevey and Montreux, and up into Alpine resorts. It is also French-speaking. Some families with ties to Geneva choose to live on the Vaud side of the lake, between Geneva and Lausanne.

## Lump-sum taxation: how the two differ

Both cantons offer lump-sum taxation (the *forfait fiscal* or *imposition d'après la dépense*) to people who are not Swiss, are coming for the first time or after ten years away, and do not work in Switzerland. The federal base of at least CHF 435,000 for 2026 applies in both. The differences are in the cantonal layer.

### Geneva

- The minimum is CHF 400,000 in law, indexed to **CHF 426,357 for 2026**.
- A **10% surcharge** is added to the base in place of wealth tax.
- You apply in writing to the cantonal tax administration on arrival, together with the migration procedure. The application includes a letter explaining your situation and confirming that you will not work in Switzerland, a **proposed lump-sum amount**, a copy of your lease or a property valuation, and a lifestyle form (*train de vie*), available in French and English.

### Vaud

- The minimum is **CHF 415,000**, and this figure already **includes a 15% surcharge** that covers wealth tax.
- The rent test is **seven times the rent plus 10%**, slightly stricter than the federal test.
- For non-EU nationals, a **signed lump-sum agreement** with the cantonal tax administration (ACI) is one of the documents required for the residence permit.

Because the base is always the highest of several tests, the minimums alone rarely tell you who pays less. A family with a large rented home may find the rent test decides the result in either canton. We look at this in [how the lump sum is calculated](/en/guides/how-lump-sum-tax-is-calculated/).

## Residence for non-EU families

EU/EFTA nationals who do not work can live in either canton on the free-movement rules, with sufficient means and health and accident insurance. For non-EU nationals, the two main routes are the retiree route from age 55 and the route based on important cantonal fiscal interests. The two cantons are open about their approach to the second:

- **Geneva** states that the major fiscal interest in the canton amounts to an expenditure base of **CHF 750,000** for third-country nationals.
- **Vaud** publishes no amount. It asks for a formal commitment not to work in Switzerland or abroad (other than managing your own assets), a copy of the lump-sum agreement with the ACI, and a land-register extract or deed if you are buying. Applicants aged 55 or over with strong ties to Switzerland may also apply as retirees.

In both cantons, the State Secretariat for Migration must approve the permit after the canton. Our guide to [residence for financially independent non-EU nationals](/en/guides/residence-non-eu-financially-independent/) explains the process.

## Inheritance and gifts

This is often the point that tips the balance for families planning ahead.

- In **Geneva**, spouses and descendants are normally exempt. However, that exemption **does not apply** if the deceased or donor was **lump-sum taxed** in one of the last three final assessments before the death or gift. For a family on the lump sum, this matters.
- In **Vaud**, descendants are not fully exempt: **CHF 1 million** is exempt, followed by a degressive deduction up to **CHF 2 million**. Spouses are exempt, as in every canton.

In both cantons, movable assets are taxed where the deceased last lived, and real estate where it is located. See our guide to [inheritance and gift tax](/en/guides/inheritance-gift-tax/).

## Buying a home

Lex Koller, the federal law on property purchases by persons abroad, is the same in both cantons. EU/EFTA nationals living here and non-EU nationals with a C permit can buy freely; non-EU nationals with a B permit can currently buy a main residence at their actual domicile. A 2026 proposal would change that, but it is not law. See [buying property as a foreigner](/en/guides/buying-property-lex-koller/).

Both cantons levy a property transfer tax, so the notary's cost estimate should be part of any offer.
