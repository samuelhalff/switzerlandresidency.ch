Switzerland has no federal wealth tax, but every canton and its communes tax the net wealth of their residents each year. As a new resident you are taxed on your worldwide assets minus debts, at rates set by your canton and commune, so the same fortune can be taxed quite differently in Zug, Geneva or Lucerne. If you are taxed on a lump sum instead, each canton has its own method for covering wealth tax.

> **Key facts (as of September 2026)**
> - Wealth tax is **cantonal and communal only**; there is **no federal wealth tax**.
> - Residents are taxed on **net worldwide wealth** (assets minus debts).
> - Rates are **mostly progressive**; **flat rates** apply in **LU, UR, SZ, OW, NW, GL, AI, SG and TG**.
> - In 2025 the threshold at which wealth tax starts (married, no children) ranged from **CHF 51,000 to CHF 410,000** by canton.
> - Under the **lump sum**, each canton decides how wealth tax is covered.
> - **Capital gains** on private securities and other movable assets are **tax-free**.

## How Swiss wealth tax works

Wealth tax is often the least familiar part of the Swiss system for new arrivals. A few principles help:

- **Who levies it.** Only the cantons and communes. The Confederation taxes income, not wealth (ESTV, *Geltende Steuern*, legal status 1 January 2026).
- **What is taxed.** Your **net wealth**: assets such as securities, bank deposits, real estate and other valuables, minus debts. As a resident with unlimited tax liability, your wealth is assessed on a **worldwide** basis.
- **Foreign real estate.** How property abroad is treated depends on the allocation rules and the relevant double tax treaty. It is a point to confirm for your own situation rather than assume.
- **Rate structure.** Most cantons use progressive rates. Nine cantons use a flat rate (see below).
- **Threshold.** Every canton exempts a first slice of wealth, but the level varies widely.

Wealth tax usually works alongside income tax. The two are assessed together in the annual return, so the overall picture depends on your whole situation, not on the wealth tax rate alone.

## Flat-rate and progressive cantons

The federal tax administration lists nine cantons with a **flat-rate** wealth tax: **Lucerne, Uri, Schwyz, Obwalden, Nidwalden, Glarus, Appenzell Innerrhoden, St. Gallen and Thurgau**. The others mostly use progressive rates, so the rate rises with the size of the fortune.

A flat rate does not automatically mean a lower bill, and a progressive rate does not automatically mean a higher one. Communal rates, the starting threshold and your income all play a part. Comparing cantons properly means running your own figures.

## When wealth tax starts: the threshold

Every canton exempts a first slice of wealth. For a married couple without children, the level at which wealth tax started in 2025 ranged from **CHF 51,000 to CHF 410,000**, depending on the canton (ESTV, *Geltende Steuern*). For the families we typically work with, the threshold is rarely decisive; the rate applied to the rest of the fortune matters much more.

## Wealth tax under the lump sum

If you qualify for [lump-sum taxation](/en/guides/swiss-lump-sum-taxation/), you are not assessed on your actual worldwide wealth in the ordinary way. Instead, each canton covers wealth tax in its own way (ESTV cantonal fact sheets, February 2026). We describe the methods in detail in [lump-sum taxation by canton](/en/guides/lump-sum-taxation-by-canton/#how-is-wealth-tax-handled-under-the-lump-sum).

Two points apply everywhere:

- The **control calculation** at cantonal level includes gross **Swiss wealth items**: Swiss real estate, movable property in Switzerland and capital invested in Switzerland (art. 6 para. 6 StHG). Large Swiss assets can therefore push the base up.
- In the five cantons that abolished the lump sum (Zurich, Schaffhausen, Appenzell Ausserrhoden, Basel-Stadt, and Basel-Landschaft outside the arrival year), everyone pays ordinary wealth tax.

## How to read the comparison

A deemed wealth multiple is taxed at the canton's own wealth-tax rates, so "20× the base" in one canton and "20× the base" in another do not produce the same tax. And the income base itself differs by canton: Zug's minimum is CHF 500,000, Schwyz's CHF 600,000 and Lucerne's CHF 647,100 for 2026.

Under ordinary taxation, the choice between a flat-rate and a progressive canton matters more as wealth grows, but so do communal rates and income tax. That is why we compare cantons on a full projection, not on a single rate. Our [lump-sum or ordinary taxation](/en/guides/lump-sum-or-ordinary-taxation/) guide shows how the two systems compare.

## Related wealth-based charges

Two other points often come up alongside wealth tax:

- **AHV/AVS for non-employed residents.** Social security contributions for people who do not work are based on **wealth plus 20 times annual pension income**. In 2026 they range from **CHF 530** to a maximum of **CHF 26,500 a year**, reached at CHF 8.95 million (AHV/IV leaflet 2.03). They are owed until the reference age of 65.
- **Capital gains.** Gains on private movable assets such as securities and art are **tax-free** at federal and cantonal level. Gains on real estate are taxed by a separate cantonal tax.

Inheritance and gift tax is also cantonal; we cover it in [inheritance and gift tax](/en/guides/inheritance-gift-tax/).
