## What is an orientation visit to Switzerland?

An orientation visit is a short, purposeful trip you take before relocating, sometimes called a look-see visit. The aim is not tourism; it is to gather the information you can only really get on the ground: how a city feels on a weekday, what a school run looks like, whether an area matches your family’s rhythm, and what the practical “admin load” will be.

For many families, the output of the trip is a short list:

- 2–3 neighbourhoods you could genuinely live in
- A schooling plan (and a realistic admissions timeline)
- A housing plan (rent first, buy later, or buy quickly)
- A relocation timeline that matches immigration and registration steps

## Why plan a look-see visit before relocating?

A look-see visit helps you avoid making “paper decisions” based on photos, headline tax comparisons, or a single weekend impression.

It is particularly useful when:

- You are undecided between several regions or cantons.
- Schooling is a priority and you need to understand commute routes and daily logistics.
- You want to sanity-check housing expectations (space, style, proximity to services).
- You are trying to understand how much can be handled remotely versus what truly needs your presence.

It also gives you a chance to understand Switzerland’s layered structure: cantons, communes, and the practical differences between them. Many relocation decisions (schools, housing, and day-to-day administration) are shaped at commune level.

## How to organise your orientation visit to Switzerland

A good plan starts with deciding what the trip needs to answer.

### Step 1: define the decisions you want to make

Common “must-answer” items:

- Where could we live that fits our lifestyle and school needs?
- Which schooling route is realistic for our child’s age and language?
- Are we aiming to rent first, or do we need to explore buying early?
- What is our likely relocation sequence after the trip?

### Step 2: build a simple itinerary with breathing space

Instead of trying to cover the whole country, focus on a small number of areas and do them properly. In practice, families often benefit from revisiting the same area at different times of day to test noise, traffic, and convenience.

### Step 3: line up the right meetings

Your meetings might include:

- A relocation agency / destination services provider
- School admissions teams (or a guided “school loop”)
- A property search partner (especially if buying is on the table)

If you are comparing school regions, our regional overview is here: [International schools in Switzerland: a regional guide](/en/guides/international-schools-switzerland/).

### Step 4: decide what to do remotely after the trip

Aim to leave Switzerland with:

- A written shortlist (areas + schools)
- A realistic move sequence
- A document list (what you will need for permits, registration, insurance, banking, etc.)

A practical follow-on is our arrival-focused checklist: [Your first 90 days in Switzerland: a new-resident checklist](/en/guides/first-90-days-checklist/).

## Which cities and regions should you explore?

There is no single “best” place; the right shortlist depends on language, schools, proximity to airports, lifestyle preferences, and how you expect to spend your time.

A helpful way to structure your comparison is by region, then by a few communes/neighbourhoods within each.

| Region | What families often test during an orientation visit |
|---|---|
| Lake Geneva area (Geneva / Vaud) | Airport access, international community, school commute patterns, lakeside vs hillside living |
| Zurich / Zug area | Pace of life, rail connections, housing style, school logistics |
| Ticino | Italian-language environment, seasonal rhythm, cross-border practicalities |
| Alpine cantons and lake towns | Travel time to airports, winter vs summer routines, second-home vs primary-home considerations |

If you already have 2 cantons in mind around Lake Geneva, this comparison can help frame the visit: [Geneva or Vaud? Choosing a home on Lake Geneva](/en/guides/geneva-or-vaud/).

You can also browse an overview of options by canton here: [Swiss cantons overview](/en/cantons/).

## What practical steps should you take during your visit?

The most valuable “practical tasks” are the ones that reduce uncertainty later.

### Housing and neighbourhood due diligence

- Walk potential neighbourhoods at school-run times.
- Test the route to the nearest station, supermarket, and sports facilities.
- If you plan to rent initially, note which areas have the right housing stock for your family.

### School planning (even if you are not applying yet)

Use the trip to clarify:

- Which campuses make sense geographically.
- How long the daily commute would be.
- What the school expects in terms of documents and timing.

(For regional school orientation, see: [International schools in Switzerland: a regional guide](/en/guides/international-schools-switzerland/).)

### Admin reality check: registration and insurance

An orientation trip is also a good moment to understand what starts when you actually move.

- For short stays (up to 90 days within 180), you do not register with the commune under the ordinary registration rules described in the SEM directives.
- If you later enter Switzerland to stay over 3 months (non-employed), commune registration is required within 14 days of entry under the SEM directives.
- Once you register as a resident, health insurance is compulsory within 3 months of registering as a resident.

These points are often easiest to absorb before you are under time pressure.

If you want the full insurance picture, see: [Swiss health insurance for new residents: a clear guide](/en/guides/health-insurance-new-residents/).

## Can you arrange meetings with relocation agencies or schools?

Yes. Many families use an orientation visit to meet a relocation agency (often called destination services in Switzerland) and to visit schools. The key is to plan ahead so you are not trying to arrange appointments once you land.

A sensible approach is:

- Share your shortlist (regions, housing needs, school priorities) in advance.
- Keep meeting slots clustered geographically.
- Leave time between appointments for neighbourhood walks and practical checks.

## What are the entry and visa rules for an orientation visit?

Entry rules for a visit depend on nationality, and Switzerland distinguishes between short stays and longer stays.

For planning purposes:

- Check visa requirements using the official SEM list of ID and visa provisions by nationality.
- For short stays of up to 90 days within 180 days, commune registration is not required under the SEM directives’ registration table.
- If your plan changes into a longer stay (over 3 months), registration obligations apply and the residence-permit process is a separate topic.

If you later hold a Swiss residence permit, it may allow travel in other Schengen states for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, but it does not give EU residence rights; other states decide their own rules.

## How does an orientation visit fit into your relocation timeline?

Think of the orientation visit as the bridge between “we are considering Switzerland” and “we have a plan we can execute”. A realistic sequence is:

- Orientation visit to narrow your shortlist and confirm practical constraints.
- Follow-up work from home: documents, applications, school process, housing strategy.
- Move and register (for stays over 3 months), then start the “first months” tasks, including health insurance within 3 months of registering.

To understand the broader sequencing (and where delays often appear), see: [How long it takes to move to Switzerland: a realistic sequence](/en/guides/swiss-residence-timeline/).

## A note on property viewings during an orientation visit

It is common to schedule property viewings during a look-see visit, especially if you are considering buying quickly.

Two points are worth keeping in mind:

- Owning property in Switzerland does not confer a right to reside.
- The rules for buying property as a foreigner can depend on your status (for example, whether you are treated as a “person abroad” under Lex Koller), so it is worth understanding the framework early.

If buying may be part of your plan, start here: [Buying property in Switzerland as a foreigner: Lex Koller](/en/guides/buying-property-lex-koller/).
